Multi-SFT: Permissively-Licensed Multilingual Instruction-Following#

Authors: Daniela Baiamonte, Kamyar Zeinalipour, Elena Fano, Stefano Simonazzi, Andrea Zugarini Venue: arXiv (2026)

Supervised fine-tuning datasets remain overwhelmingly English-centric and commercially restricted, as their content is frequently generated by proprietary models whose terms of service forbid training competing systems. We introduce Multi-SFT, a commercially permissive collection of 502,818 instruction-response pairs across five European languages, built via translation and regeneration pipelines for tasks where translation is insufficient. Human evaluation yields inter-annotator agreement of at least 0.87 across all datasets and languages. Fine-tuning nine models from three different families (EuroLLM, OLMo 2, Qwen3; 1B-32B) and evaluating them on 13 benchmarks shows that Multi-SFT alone is competitive with larger augmented mixes and official instruction-tuned releases, with decisive advantages on safety tasks.

The Right Byte First: How UTF-16 Helps Multilingual Boundary Prediction#

Authors: Hyunsik Kim Venue: arXiv (2026)

Many hierarchical byte-level language models group bytes into patches using a learned boundary predictor whose view of the next codepoint depends on encoding and lookahead. In UTF-8, codepoints of many scripts take three bytes: 8 of 24 bits mark length and continuation, and the first byte carries only 4 high codepoint bits. After the same left context, a mark extending a grapheme cluster and a letter starting the next can thus share their first byte. Universal Byte-Level Encoding (UBE) keeps one- and two-byte UTF-8 encodings but writes these codepoints as two UTF-16 bytes, low byte first, so its first symbol carries 8 low codepoint bits that can distinguish such continuations. We train small causal predictors on matched FineWeb-2 text and evaluate on FLORES-200 under UTF-8, UBE, and UBE’s same-length big-endian variant. At one-symbol lookahead, balanced grapheme-boundary error at codepoint boundaries across 17 languages in 13 Brahmic scripts is 8.76% with UTF-8, 8.62% with the big-endian variant, and 0.06% with UBE. When the target instead retains a parent tokenizer’s subword cuts at grapheme boundaries, UBE lowers the mean error relative to UTF-8 at the same lookahead by 72.91% in Korean, 35.29% in Chinese, and 67.32-99.55% in eight more scripts. In the seven of them whose main Unicode block fits on one 256-codepoint page, the big-endian variant stays within 3.0% of UTF-8, so the gain follows byte order, not length. In Korean and Chinese, UBE’s mean error falls below UTF-8’s final mean error after only 25% and 37.5% of training. Which byte comes first thus affects how much lookahead a byte-level boundary predictor needs.

Priming Base Models for Agentic Post-Training: An Open Midtraining Recipe#

Authors: Soumye Singhal, Joseph Jennings, Ka Shun Shum, Alexander Bukharin, Khushi Bhardwaj, Zirui Liu, Venkat Srinivasan, Jonathan Li, Yash Jain, Kezhi Kong, Markus Kliegl, Olivier Delalleau, Sanjeev Satheesh, Mostofa Patwary Venue: arXiv (2026)

Frontier models, general-purpose and domain-specific alike, now include a midtraining stage between pretraining and post-training, but little has been published about how this stage is built. We present an open midtraining recipe that primes base models for agentic post-training, and we show its effectiveness on two Nemotron models of different sizes. Pretraining typically runs at short context and sees almost no agent interactions, while SFT data is mostly synthetic, and very long SFT stages can hurt the model’s later RL trainability. Our midtraining stage sits between the two. It trains at 256K context for hundreds of billions of tokens on a diverse mixture of natural software-engineering data like packed repositories, pull requests, commit histories, and build logs together with a small share of agentic traces. On Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, 500B midtraining tokens improve SWE-bench Verified from 60.7 to 64.5, SWE-bench Multilingual from 55.8 to 62.8, and SWE-bench Pro from 32.0 to 35.5 after identical full SFT. On the larger Nemotron 3.5 Super, 280B midtraining tokens improve DeepSWE, a harder benchmark of long-horizon tasks, from 11.7 to 15.7 and SWE-bench Verified from 72.9 to 74.3 after identical full SFT. Midtraining also improves robustness across agent harnesses on both models. We report the ablations behind the recipe and an evaluation protocol built on short fixed SFT runs, since base-model proxy metrics did not correlate reliably with post-training results.

Learning Speech Attribute Representations via Multi-Teacher Distillation#

Authors: Maryem Bouziane, Salima Mdhaffar, Yannick Estève Venue: arXiv (2026)

Recent post-training approaches have shown that self-supervised speech encoders can be adapted to learn a single utterance-level speech attribute through knowledge distillation. In this work, we investigate whether a single speech encoder can simultaneously preserve multiple complementary utterance-level speech representations. We propose a multi-attribute multi-teacher distillation framework that learns semantic, speaker, language, and emotion representations from four frozen teacher models using a shared SSL backbone and attribute-specific branches. Experiments on multilingual semantic retrieval, speaker verification, language identification, and speech emotion recognition show that the proposed model achieves performance close to dedicated single-task students while requiring only a single shared encoder. Furthermore, the learned layer aggregation strategies reveal that different speech attributes naturally emerge at different depths of the SSL encoder, providing new insights into the organization of speech representations.

Behavioral Forgetting Is Not Privacy in Machine Unlearning#

Authors: Tyler Lizzo, Larry Heck Venue: arXiv (2026)

Machine unlearning aims to remove targeted information from large language models, but existing evaluations often measure forgetting only on a designated benchmark distribution. We argue that such behavioral forgetting is an insufficient standard for privacy-oriented unlearning, and that evaluation should instead test whether forgetting persists across the access paths through which the same information may be recovered. We demonstrate this with a controlled case study of cross-lingual querying: across multiple multilingual model families, forgetting varies substantially when semantically equivalent information is queried in different languages and scripts. A single access path is therefore enough to expose a real gap between behavioral forgetting and information inaccessibility, motivating broader evaluation of access-path generalization and adversarial recoverability alongside in-distribution forgetting.

Pairwise AUC Optimization Needs Corrective Power: A Unified View#

Authors: Jia Chen, Zhiyong Yang, Shilong Bao, Qianqian Xu, Qingming Huang Venue: arXiv (2026)

Direct pairwise AUC-surrogate training is attractive because it trains toward the ranking metric used at test time, especially under class imbalance. This creates a direct alignment between the training objective and the evaluation metric, but such metric alignment alone does not guarantee that stochastic training remains corrective. We study this gap through Corrective Power: whether pairwise mini-batch updates produce stronger and more reliable improvements for more severe positive-negative ranking errors. Our main observation is that direct pairwise training can enter regions where severe errors remain common but no longer produce useful corrective motion. In such regions, even the mini-batch noise that would normally help SGD move along the steepest severe-error correction direction can become much weaker. In this paper, we seek a theoretical explanation for this by exploring the degenerate property of the U-statistics formed from the pairwise gradients therein. Our results provide a mechanistic explanation for several empirical patterns: (a) squared-hinge loss is often an effective AUC surrogate; (b) cross-entropy warm-up can help models that enter the AUC phase from a poor starting region; and (c) parameter-efficient fine-tuning from a pretrained model can reduce that need by starting closer to a favorable ranking geometry. Experiments on 8 CV tasks and 8 NLP tasks, using ResNet50, DenseNet121, DistilBERT-base, and Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, support these predictions.

Wolof-Adapted Industry-Ready Open-Weight Language Models#

Wolof is spoken by 23M people, mostly in Senegal, but remains weakly supported by open-weight language models, while API models raise cost, governance, and sovereignty constraints for local deployments. In this paper, we present 0.6B-14B open-weight models adapted to Wolof through instruction tuning, preference alignment, and model merging. PRISM: Transfer and Limits of Activation Interventions in Tool-Calling Language Models#

Authors: Giovana Kerche Bonás, Thales Sales Almeida, Marcelo da Silva Reis, Rodrigo Nogueira, Marcos Meideiros Raimundo Venue: arXiv (2026)

Recent literature shows that unsafe tool calls can often accompany textual refusals in language models. To investigate this hidden vulnerability, we introduce PRISM, a framework for evaluating the transfer of activation interventions across precision, languages, and scales. Our findings demonstrate that leveraging lower-resource settings (specifically quantized NF4 labels and cross-lingual transfer) offers a robust pathway to expose and improve model alignment. Notably, using behavioral labels from an NF4 copy enables stable intervention estimation even with very few scenarios. Furthermore, these alignment improvements transfer effectively across languages: five-language NF4-supervised directions successfully transfer to held-out Arabic, reducing unsafe violations in Llama-3.1-8B by 21.95 percentage points without target-language tuning. By mapping paired benign activations, we also successfully transfer these safety interventions to much larger models (up to 70B parameters). Ultimately, PRISM highlights how auxiliary supervision from compressed models and multilingual data can uncover and mitigate hazardous tool-calling behaviors across different model families. We release all experimental code and supporting artifacts at https://github.com/GiKerche/prism, alongside an interactive explorer of recorded model responses and experimental results at https://gikerche.github.io/prism/.

Evaluating Large Language Models for Multilingual Abstractive Summarization: A Comprehensive Study of English and Bengali#

Authors: Khan Mahi Al Atahar, Nazifa Tasneem, Ajwad Abrar Venue: arXiv (2026)

Abstractive summarization remains challenging across languages, where linguistic quality and factual consistency may diverge, particularly in low-resource settings. We present a standardized zero-shot benchmark of eight closed-source and open-weight LLMs on size-matched English and Bengali XL-Sum test sets. Our evaluation combines lexical and semantic metrics with reference-free factuality verification and multidimensional LLM-as-a-Judge assessment, providing a comprehensive analysis of both surface-level quality and factual reliability in multilingual summarization. Results reveal substantial cross-lingual variation: GPT-5.6 Luna leads several English lexical metrics but does not maintain the same advantage in Bengali, where LLaMA 3.3 70B achieves the highest ROUGE and BLEU scores. Gemini 2.5 Flash obtains the highest observed Bengali factuality score. Furthermore, evaluation methods produce different model rankings, highlighting the limitations of relying on a single metric for multilingual summarization assessment. These findings demonstrate that strong English summarization performance does not necessarily transfer to Bengali and emphasize the need for comprehensive evaluation frameworks for low-resource languages.

Probing the Geometry of Color Representations in Multilingual Language Models#

Authors: Pablo Loyola, Edison Marrese-Taylor, Andrés Hoyos-Idrobo Venue: arXiv (2026)

Do multilingual language models learn a universal internal geometry for color, and is it perceptually grounded or primarily linguistic? We decompose color representations into two geometric axes: (P1) alignment to perceptual color space, and (P2) cross-lingual symmetry. Analyzing layerwise embeddings across models and multilingual datasets, we find that perceptual grounding is weak: alignment to CIE-LAB peaks in intermediate layers, then collapses toward output layers. Cross-lingual symmetry degrades with depth, suggesting later layers encode language-specific structure. Functionally, we show that geometric properties predict task performance: models achieve 96.7% accuracy on color classification at layers where P1 alignment peaks, with P1 alignment (r=0.70) significantly correlating with downstream capabilities. Our results reveal a dissociation: text-only LMs learn universal color-language geometry, not universal color-perception geometry, providing diagnostics for where perceptual structure is preserved in language models.

A Benchmark for Bangla Task-Oriented Dialogue Understanding#

Authors: Afrina Alam Lamia, Sumiaya Sharif, Prottoy Modak, Ohiduzzaman Rayhan, Nafis Sadeq Venue: arXiv (2026)

Task-oriented dialogue understanding remains underexplored for Bangla, particularly in multilingual settings involving English and romanized Bangla (Banglish). We introduce BanglaTOD, a parallel tri-lingual benchmark comprising 209 multi-turn dialogues across nine Bangladesh-specific service domains and 64 intents. Each conversational turn is aligned across Bangla, English, and Banglish, yielding 4,689 language-specific turn records annotated with intents, token-level slots, state updates, and accumulated dialogue states. We establish context-aware baselines for joint intent classification and slot filling using mBERT, BERT-base-uncased, and BanglaBERT. Cross-language analysis reveals substantial performance differences across models and linguistic representations, while removing dialogue history reduces intent accuracy by 19.0–25.7 percentage points. BanglaTOD provides a foundation for multilingual dialogue understanding and future dialogue state tracking research in Bangladesh-specific conversational applications. The dataset is publicly available at \href{https://huggingface.co/datasets/afri7here/BanglaTOD}{Hugging Face}.

Multi-sourced AI Tutor for Non-Native English Speaker Students#

Authors: Yiyin Shen, Xinyi Ai, Weibing Wang, Rogers Jeffrey Leo John, Meenakshi Syamkumar Venue: arXiv (2026)

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being adopted as teaching tools in computing education, particularly for developing AI tutors capable of answering student questions. With the rise in enrollment of international students in computing programs, researchers have begun to explore the difficulties that non-native English speaker (NNES) students face in computing courses due to language barriers. To aid such students, we developed a general-purpose AI tutor system that can process student questions in foreign languages (currently focused on Mandarin) and provide bilingual responses. Our system also incorporates the functionality of generating a glossary of technical terms and their explanations to enhance comprehension. Our approach utilizes a specialized Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline with a novel question extraction mechanism, enabling us to effectively utilize a diverse array of course materials for answering student queries. We evaluated our AI tutor using 91 Piazza posts, demonstrating that it could answer 83.52% of the posts correctly and in a helpful manner. The tutor also provided high-quality translations for 76.92% of the questions and generated technical term glossaries for 64.47% of them. Our findings underscore the potential of multi-source RAG pipeline in enhancing AI tutor systems, offering support for non-native speakers that are challenging to provide with traditional supporting staff of a computing course.

When Does “Post-Call” Begin? An Availability Contract for Earnings-Call NLP Benchmarks#

Authors: Xiaoyu Ma, Kelvin J. L. Koa, Yuncong Liu, Ke-Wei Huang Venue: arXiv (2026)

Corporate financial disclosures often unfold over time, with announcements, prepared remarks, and discussion spanning multiple high-frequency return intervals. Financial NLP benchmarks can therefore suffer from a within-event timing mismatch when the prediction target begins before the textual or audio input used by a model could be complete. Chronological train–test splits do not prevent this mismatch, because it occurs inside individual events rather than across samples. We introduce an Availability Contract, which requires a benchmark to state the input boundary, evidence for input availability, the outcome start, and the claims supported by that evidence. We apply this framework to 552 earnings calls from 24 firms. For each call, we hold the return endpoint fixed and compare the previous close, a documentary call-start proxy, and a completion proxy based on reconstructed audio end plus a stipulated processing allowance. At this baseline, moving from the call-start proxy to the completion proxy flips return signs for 9.4% of calls. Holding completion-clock cutpoints fixed, 28.3% change quintile. In an independent release-session analysis of a public earnings-call volatility benchmark, shifting before-market target windows forward by one trading session raises three-day mean squared error by 18.1-21.4% across four frozen prediction streams, while their point rankings remain unchanged. Together, the results show that labels and losses can depend materially on the declared outcome clock even when model rankings appear stable. The Availability Contract makes this assumption explicit and ties post-input claims to the available timing evidence.

Personalized Projected Ascent for Federated Stackelberg Optimization#

Authors: M Yashwanth, Sharannya Ghosh, Saikiran Bulusu, Anirban Chakraborty Venue: arXiv (2026)

Federated minimax problems, such as distributionally robust optimization and adversarial training, include an inner maximization variable built from each client’s local data. These variables differ in meaning and dimension from one client to the next, so averaging them across clients, as standard federated minimax methods do, is ill-posed. We therefore study a federated minimax problem where the minimization variable $\mathbf{x}$ is shared and aggregated, while each client retains the maximization variable $\mathbf{y}_i$ (personalized). Because these applications constrain $\mathbf{y}_i$ to a compact convex set, we analyze projected ascent directly. We show that, in the nonconvex-concave regime, this projected personalized scheme attains the standard sublinear rate. In the nonconvex-PL regime, however, it hits a stepsize-invariant noise floor due to stochastic $\mathbf{y}_i$ updates. To mitigate this, we propose VR-SGDA, incorporating a variance-reduced ascent step where each client maintains a private variable snapshot. By evaluating current and snapshot gradients on the same sample, the constant variance is replaced by a vanishing drift under a two-timescale regime. VR-SGDA eliminates the noise floor, attaining sublinear leading rates while preserving personalization and projection. Experiments on subpopulation-robustness and NLP adversarial training confirm both the worst-group robustness benefits and the two-timescale tracking mechanism.

CoSTA: Compressed Speech Token Adaptation for Efficient Spoken Dialogue Models#

Authors: Yingzhuo Deng, Chong Li, Bingxin Liu, Yi Shu, Tianyu Peng, Can Xie, Siyao Song, Shuo Ren, Jiajun Zhang Venue: arXiv (2026)

Spoken Dialogue Models (SDMs) typically share a speech-token space for generation and synthesis, leading to long autoregressive sequences and costly inference. Lower-rate speech tokenizers shorten sequences but may introduce compression-quality trade-offs and costly replacement without breaking this coupling. To address this limitation, we propose CoSTA, a compatibility-preserving post-hoc framework that decouples generation and synthesis token spaces while retaining the original speech tokenizer and decoder. CoSTA extends the generation token space with compressed representations via mean-initialized vocabulary extension, pronunciation adaptation, and dialogue adaptation, followed by deterministic expansion back to the original speech-token space before synthesis. On bilingual speech-dialogue benchmarks, CoSTA achieves approximately $1.7\times$ expanded speech-token generation speed, lower full-response latency, and higher throughput while preserving response quality. Analyses show more sustained interaction under limited budgets and a practical trade-off among compression, decoding efficiency, and quality. Further experiments demonstrate cross-architecture transfer and complementarity to low-rate tokenization.

PremiseCheck: Efficient Fact-Checking of Human and AI Peer Reviews#

Authors: Dingjie Song, Zhiling Yan, Ziqian Zhang, Yuxuan Zhang, Lichao Sun Venue: arXiv (2026)

Peer review is under strain from the growth of submissions, and review comments written by humans or AI can misstate what a paper says. We introduce ReviewFact, a benchmark that labels such factual errors in review comments under one definition across NLP, machine learning, and the natural sciences, and that measures cost and latency alongside accuracy. A frontier LLM checks comments well but must read the whole paper for each check, while cheap decision models, which score whether a statement holds in a text, fall far short on whole comments that mix several implicit assertions. PremiseCheck is a framework that combines the two. An LLM rewrites each comment as explicit premises without reading the paper, and a decision model checks each premise against the paper. When each comment is checked on its own, PremiseCheck reaches accuracy comparable to GPT-5.5 across all three fields at about half its cost, and on NLP reviews at a quarter of its cost and less than half its latency.

Magna: simulating magic state factories as cheaply as Clifford circuits#

Authors: Yifei Wang, Yingfei Gu Venue: arXiv (2026)

Classical simulation of magic state factories, including distillation, cultivation, and code switching, is challenging because of their substantial non-Clifford content. We introduce $\texttt{magna}$ (magic-state-factory assessment via global network algebra), a classical simulator that simulates such protocols as cheaply as Clifford circuits. $\texttt{magna}$ expresses the figures of merit of a protocol, namely the acceptance rate and the fidelity of the accepted states, as tensor networks, which it reduces globally by exploiting a structural correspondence with transversal gates on quantum codes. $\texttt{magna}$ further supports feedforward conditioned on arbitrary functions of mid-circuit measurement outcomes, including decoder outputs. We simulate Bravyi–Haah distillation with up to 100 output magic states at a cost that does not grow exponentially in the number of outputs, and achieve state-of-the-art sampling times for distance-5 magic state cultivation on both color codes and RP$^2$ surface codes. Finally, we validate nonlinear feedforward on a logical-level distillation protocol with 15 rounds of decoder-dependent feedforward. $\texttt{magna}$ aims to contribute to the key infrastructure for the large-scale evaluation and optimization of magic state factories.

ARC-SORT: Adaptive, Robust, Compensated Multi-Object Tracking with Interacting Multiple Models#

Authors: Girish Kumar Adari Venue: arXiv (2026)

Multi-object tracking in sports broadcasts is hard for two reasons: athletes move fast and change direction abruptly, and the camera pans and zooms at the same time. We present ARC-SORT (Adaptive, Robust, Compensated SORT), a tracking-by-detection tracker that trains nothing and runs in real time. Its design rule: the tracker measures its own uncertainty, and that measurement decides how it acts. An Interacting Multiple Model (IMM) Kalman filter gives every track a predictive covariance, and we turn it into a per-track search region: the less reliable the motion prediction, the larger the region in which a detection may match (uncertainty-adaptive Expansion IoU, EIoU). On identical inputs, this continuous coupling is what matters: the same covariance used as a hard Mahalanobis gate hurts at every threshold we swept, and richer covariance summaries add nothing measurable. When two tracks cross, the assignment cost itself reports whether the decision was close. ARC-SORT keeps such pairs under review and applies at most one corrective identity swap once post-crossing appearance evidence shows the first decision was wrong; this adds +1.0 HOTA (higher order tracking accuracy) on both the SportsMOT and DanceTrack test sets at under a millisecond per frame. For players who leave the frame for seconds, motion extrapolation carries no information, so our offline re-identification drops the usual motion gate and links tracklets by appearance alone. ARC-SORT reaches 81.82 HOTA on the SportsMOT test set with the fewest identity switches of the 69 leaderboard entries as of August 2026 and tied-third multi-object tracking accuracy (MOTA, 97.09), and 62.1 HOTA on DanceTrack test with the same association settings. On Deep-EIoU’s public detections and embeddings it reaches 80.31 HOTA, +3.1 over Deep-EIoU. It runs at about 50 frames per second on a laptop RTX 3060 GPU. Code will be released.

Patch where the text is new: a compression view of where byte-level models should spend compute#

Authors: Nicolás Vera Zúñiga Venue: arXiv (2026)

Byte-level language models such as the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) run their large global model once per patch and start a patch where a small model’s next-byte entropy is high. This rule rarely starts a patch at in-line computed results, such as the number after “=” in a worked solution, and accuracy on them collapses under tight patch budgets. We propose novelty patching, motivated by LZ77-style compression: start a patch where the upcoming bytes are unlikely to repeat the recent context.

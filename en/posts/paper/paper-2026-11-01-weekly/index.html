Machine Learning-Based Detection of Mobile Money Scan Messages in Ghana: A Comparative Evaluation of Naive Bayes, Support Vector Machine, and Randon Forest Classifiers#

Authors: Ibrahim Salifu, Muhaisin Tiyumba Nantogmah Venue: arXiv (2026)

Mobile money (MoMo) is Ghana’s preferred retail payment channel, recording GHC518.4 billion in transactions in December 2025 and over 26.7 million active accounts (Graphic Online, 2026). This growth has coincided with increased social-engineering fraud over Short Message Service (SMS) and Unstructured Supplementary Service Data (USSD), where fraudsters impersonate network operators, agents, relatives, or regulators to obtain personal identification numbers (PINs), identity data, or funds. We present a supervised machine-learning pipeline for classifying scam and legitimate Ghanaian MoMo SMS messages using a labelled corpus of 220 messages across 18 scam and legitimate subcategories. Domain-specific normalisation of currency amounts, phone numbers, and USSD codes preceded TF-IDF vectorisation of unigrams and bigrams. Using stratified five-fold cross-validation and a 20% held-out test set, we trained and evaluated Multinomial Naive Bayes, linear support vector machine (SVM), and Random Forest classifiers using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). Held-out accuracy, F1, and AUC were 1.00 for Naive Bayes and linear SVM; cross-validated accuracy averaged 96.6% (+/-3.3), 94.9% (+/-4.6), and 92.0% (+/-4.2). Discriminative n-grams reflected imperative urgency (“send,” “claim,” “please send”) and PIN solicitation (“PIN,” “pincode”); legitimate messages featured confirmatory and cautionary language (“balance,” “successful,” “do not share”). Findings are contextualised in Sub-Saharan African SMS fraud detection, small template-based corpus ceiling effects, and a pathway toward larger naturalistic multilingual corpora. Interpretable classical classifiers achieve high accuracy on structured MoMo scam text and suit carrier- or handset-level filtering in low-resource, on-device settings.

IndicIPR-QA: A Benchmark for Extractive Question Answering on Indian IPR Laws#

Authors: M. Mohaiminul Islam, Muhammad Abulaish Venue: arXiv (2026)

Legal Question Answering (QA) over statutory texts is a challenging task in Natural Language Processing (NLP), especially in low-resource settings with complex legal systems. It requires answers that are precise and grounded in the law. However, to the best of current knowledge, no existing extractive QA benchmark provides exact answer spans from statutory texts across all seven Indian Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) Acts. This paper presents IndicIPR-QA1, an extractive QA benchmark for Indian IPR laws. The proposed dataset contains 4,462 QA pairs from 1,116 statutory contexts across seven Acts. Questions and answers are generated using GPT-4o and validated through nine automatic quality checks, single and multi-model LLM evaluations, and a 200-pair human audit. Section-level partitioning prevents source-context overlap across the training, validation, and test sets. Further analysis examines answer span length, lexical overlap, and multi-clause structure across the corpus and individual Acts. BM25 achieves a token-level F1 score of 20.21, while fine-tuned InLegalBERT reaches 85.02 under a unified sliding-window inference protocol. The large performance gap between BM25 (20.21 F1) and InLegalBERT (85.02 F1) shows that simple word matching is not enough to answer these legal questions. Performance also varies across the seven Acts, indicating substantial differences in answer-extraction performance.

How I Aligned Your Model: Scaling Up Multilingual Safety Training with Templates#

Authors: Max Neuwinger, Hanna Yukhymenko, Imanol Schlag Venue: arXiv (2026)

Safety alignment for Large Language Models is heavily focused on English with limited multilingual representation, particularly damaging low-resource language performance. Related open training datasets are often limited in size or do not follow region-localized harmful topics to broaden the scope of alignment beyond cultures. We propose a scalable template-based augmentation pipeline to produce massive multilingual refusal and benign corpora, extracting safe templates from harmful prompts, translating them and then filling in the slots with culturally authentic entities, both harmful and benign. As a result, we release MSafeTemp, a validated corpus spanning 60 languages useful for multilingual safety alignment and evaluation. Using the dataset, we show that English-only evaluation misjudges multilingual safety: for several models, attacks that fail in English succeed once translated, while culturally localized variants expose further failures that translation alone misses. We also confirm that augmenting safety training data through templates allows for higher diversity and quality of alignment, resulting in improved safety performance beyond English. MSafeTemp and the respective pipeline are aimed at facilitating research and development of safer and more responsible models for less-represented cultures.

rTaste: Do Research Agents Have Taste? Evaluating Early Stage Research Judgment#

Authors: Art Kanke Venue: arXiv (2026)

Do research agents have taste, the judgment of which experiments are worth running before their outcomes are known? We give six frontier models seven recent NLP papers. Each model, working as a research agent alone or as a team, proposes minimum viable experiments (MVEs), forecasts whether each will pass a threshold it sets and ranks them. A separate executor then attempts every MVE, including those ranked last, and three model auditors judge the results. Of 415 MVEs, 194 are judged sound, valuable and new, but fewer than half of these (73) are also judged beyond the seed paper and non obvious. The agents’ first choices match the auditors’ most valued experiment more often than a random pick, but are not shown to improve on choosing the experiment written first. Their forecasts beat a base rate but are not measurably more accurate than those of a fresh instance of the same model given only the paper and registrations. Teams cost more without a clear overall improvement in proposal quality. The agents produce many worthwhile experiments, but the evidence that their rankings improve the choice of which to run remains limited. The evaluation harness and a dataset of all 415 MVEs, with their forecasts and audited outcomes, are available at https://github.com/ArtKanke/rTaste.

Towards Full-Text Scholarly Argument Mining using Entity-Anchored Claim Extraction#

Authors: Bhuvanesh Verma, Andy Lüking, Mounika Marreddy, Leon Lukas Hammerla, Alexander Mehler Venue: arXiv (2026)

Argument mining in full-text scholarly articles remains an important yet challenging task. This difficulty stems not only from the need to model long-distance dependencies but also from the substantial effort required to annotate sentence-level argumentative components throughout lengthy documents. To address these challenges, we introduce a novel approach that leverages scientific entity mentions (e.g., tasks, datasets, and models) to identify and structure claims in scholarly literature. We validate our approach through a case study involving 50 full-text research papers on sentiment analysis. We first establish a reliable human gold standard (Krippendorff’s $α= 0.80$), identifying 113 claim-bearing sentences and extracting 199 structured claim records from 300 candidate sentences. To address the annotation bottleneck, we employ a majority-voting ensemble of large language models (LLMs) to generate a silver-standard dataset comprising 1,487 claims. Encoders fine-tuned exclusively on the silver-standard dataset outperform those trained on a small human-annotated gold-standard dataset and demonstrate strong zero-shot transfer to NLP papers outside the training corpus.

Towards Multilingual End-of-Turn Detection using Frozen Speech Encoders#

Authors: Rini Sharon, Kadri Hacioglu, Andreas Stolcke Venue: arXiv (2026)

A spoken dialogue system must judge when the user has yielded the floor. This end-of-turn (EoT) detector is normally trained and tuned afresh for each language, which is costly to scale to new languages. We instead build one on a frozen multilingual encoder, training only a small fixed-size head. Using five CALLHOME languages, we investigate three questions. First, how well a head trained on one language transfers zero-shot to the others. Second, how much target-language adaptation is required to recover full task performance. Third, whether a single fixed decision threshold holds across languages without per-language tuning. We find that the head transfers well, even between tonal and pitch-accent languages, and that a few hours of audio recover nearly all the loss, matching in-language training. A frozen multilingual encoder with one lightweight head is a practical, language-agnostic baseline for EoT detection.

Red-Teaming Large Language Models in Low-Resource African Languages: A Case Study of Yoruba and Nigerian Pidgin#

Authors: Paula Chikaodinaka Anyanwu, Valdini Douglace Lemofouet, Blessing Ngozi Uzor, Ifeoluwa Dorcas Awotunde, Danielle Blanche Kapsa, Tassallah Abdullahi, Clemencia Siro, Idris Abdulmumin, Seid Muhie Yimam, Shamsuddeen Hassan Muhammad Venue: arXiv (2026)

As large language models (LLMs) become more widespread in Africa, their safety in low-resource languages remains largely untested since most red-teaming work focuses on English. This study evaluates six state-of-the-art LLMs using 400 explicit adversarial prompts in Yoruba and Nigerian Pidgin, categorised into four harm categories. The responses were scored by native-speaker annotators and reliability was assessed using Cohen’s $κ$. While models that rejected up to 69% of English prompts rejected as few as 0% in Yoruba, this was not because they were safer, but because they failed to understand the prompts. Attack success rates increased for most models in the Nigerian languages, with GPT-4o Mini demonstrating the most significant cross-lingual safety discrepancy (+0.23 in Yoruba). When models did engage with adversarial prompts, many of the responses were harmful, with rates of harmful content reaching 0.63 in English and 0.50 in Nigerian Pidgin. The most difficult harm category to detect was cultural stereotypes and discrimination (ASR = 0.44 in Nigerian Pidgin). Comprehension in Yoruba was low across most models, and this directly suppressed apparent attack success without indicating actual safety. Gemini 2.0 Flash was the most consistent model across languages. Despite its explicit training in African languages, Aya 101 had the highest attack success rate in English (0.60) and one of the lowest Yoruba comprehension rates (0.28). These results demonstrate that English-centric safety alignment does not transfer to Nigerian languages, highlighting the urgent need for language-specific safety evaluations.

Leaving the Cave by Breaking the Symmetry: Identifiable Structure Can Enable Cross-Lingual Generalization#

Authors: Frederick Riemenschneider, Anette Frank Venue: arXiv (2026)

Why do multilingual language models generalize across languages even without shared vocabulary? The Platonic Representation Hypothesis posits that models trained on different data, objectives, and modalities converge toward a shared statistical model of reality. Extending this intuition to representations inside a single multilingual model, we propose the Relational Identifiability Hypothesis: relations in a shared world can promote entity alignment by ruling out alternative cross-lingual correspondences, making corresponding entities more likely to become directly readable without an additional transformation. We test this hypothesis in a controlled bilingual Transformer whose two token-disjoint languages describe the same modular-arithmetic world. We find that the languages can develop a shared answer subspace without aligning corresponding numbers. Cross-lingual alignment emerges selectively when the available relations constrain number correspondences: subtraction makes zero task-level identifiable, inducing selective alignment, while an order-sensitive operation produces broad alignment across numbers. These patterns predict success in zero-shot cross-lingual transfer. Together, our results show that relational structure in a shared world can promote entity alignment and enable transfer.

Cross Lingual Abstractive Summarization for Marathi#

Authors: Asang Dani, Shailesh R Sathe Venue: arXiv (2026)

Text summarization is an important task for advancing natural language processing (NLP) research and applications in Indic languages. Despite progress in extractive and abstractive summarization, both monolingual summarization (MS) and cross-lingual summarization (CLS) remain relatively underexplored for these languages. Recent advances in Transformer architectures, pretrained language models (PLMs), and dataset availability offer new opportunities to address this gap. In this work, we construct cross-lingual training data from the monolingual Marathi MahaSum corpus and combine it with the smaller cross-lingual PMIndiaSum dataset to fine-tune IndicTrans2, an Indic neural machine translation model. We evaluate the resulting models using ROUGE, chrF and chrF++ metrics, focusing primarily on Marathi-to-English summarization due to computational constraints. We also discuss the applicability of our approach to the English-to-Marathi direction. Our experiments demonstrate that leveraging a pretrained translation model yields substantially better summarization performance than the IndicBART and mBART-50 based baselines reported in previous work on cross-lingual summarization.

Japanese Discourse Structure Analysis Based on Segmented Discourse Representation Theory Using LLMs#

Authors: Satomu Tomisaki, Yosuke Mikami, Amane Watahiki, Hitomi Yanaka Venue: arXiv (2026)

Discourse parsing based on Segmented Discourse Representation Theory (SDRT) is crucial for capturing the structural dynamics of dialogues. However, most prior research focuses on English, and its effectiveness for Japanese remains unclear owing to the lack of Japanese SDRT corpora. To address this issue, we construct Japanese SDRT corpora by translating existing English SDRT corpora using an LLM. Through a comparative evaluation of prompt-based and fine-tuned LLMs, we show that the models achieve comparable performance between English and Japanese, suggesting the cross-lingual applicability of SDRT-based discourse parsing. In order to investigate why the English and Japanese parsers achieve similar performance, we conduct ablation analyses on discourse markers and contextual inputs. Our results reveal that most of the Japanese discourse markers we examined have little impact, and the models’ predictions depend heavily on contextual discourse relations rather than on the content of the context clauses. This structural reliance might explain the comparable performance despite the substantial typological differences between English and Japanese.

No Tokenizer, Still a Gap: Cross-Lingual Fairness of Homoglyph and Invisible-Character Watermarking#

Authors: Alexander Nemecek, Yuqiao Xu, Joseph Falkenburg, Erman Ayday Venue: arXiv (2026)

Homoglyph and invisible-character watermarks are edit-based watermarks. Instead of changing how a model generates text, they hide a signal in the text after it is written. They swap some characters for homoglyphs, which look the same, or very similar, but have different Unicode code points, or they insert characters that do not display, all without access to the model that wrote the text. Generation-time watermarks work less well for lower-resourced languages due to how tokenizers split their text. Because they work on individual characters, these watermarks never touch a tokenizer, so we ask whether they escape this gap. Across 40 language varieties in 24 scripts, we measure capacity (the hidden bits each character can carry), how much of it survives routine handling and targeted removal, and how much text a keyed detector needs. Capacity varies 3.3-fold and script accounts for 96% of that variation. Within Latin script, resource level makes no detectable difference ($ρ=+0.095$, $p=0.78$), but across scripts capacity rises with it ($ρ=+0.54$, 95% CI $[+0.14,+0.83]$), because lower-resourced languages tend to use scripts with few homoglyphs. Ordinary copy-paste, which strips invisible characters, widens the gap: a detector then needs about 2 code points of English or Persian but 93 of Lao. Removing the tokenizer does not remove the inequity, but instead only changes its source.

MATCHA: A Chinese Multi-Disorder Mental Health Dataset with Cross-Lingual Analyses on Social Media#

Authors: Bingrui Jin, Baihan Li, Zitong Li, Kunyao Lan, Shimeng Li, Kenny Q. Zhu, Mengyue Wu Venue: arXiv (2026)

Mental health research on social media has been dominated by English-language datasets and DSM-5-centered resources, limiting cross-lingual and cross-cultural understanding of online mental health expression. We introduce MATCHA, the first Chinese multi-disorder mental health dataset that links disorder-specific Weibo Super-Topic communities with users’ broader public timelines. MATCHA covers seven DSM-5 disorders and a control group, comprising 46,025 cohort-level users and 1.8 million posts. The dataset distinguishes users with explicit self-disclosure evidence from broader disorder-related participants through an LLM-assisted annotation pipeline, and is released in a rigorously de-identified, controlled-access format. Using MATCHA, we analyze behavioral, linguistic, symptom-related, and life-event-related patterns across disorders, and conduct cross-lingual comparisons with Twitter-STMHD. Our analyses show that Weibo Super-Topics function as concentrated spaces for affective and health-related expression, while cross-platform differences between Weibo and Twitter often exceed cross-disorder variation. MATCHA provides a new resource for community-aware and cross-lingual mental health NLP research.

Toward Complete Speaker Anonymization: a Streaming Cascaded System for Voice Privacy and PII Removal#

Authors: Stanisław Kacprzak, Thomas Thebaud, Najim Dehak Venue: arXiv (2026)

We present a cascade streaming voice privacy system, consisting of ASR, NLP, and TTS modules. By leveraging recent improvements in all three aspects of speech and language processing, we show that such a system is capable of achieving a result comparable to state-of-the-art offline voice privacy systems, while providing controllable capabilities of content protection beyond voice. With an estimated utterance-onset end-to-end latency of 717 ms, the presented system preserves speech content with 11% WER on Librispeech, while detecting PII with a 69% SLU F1 score and achieving up to 47.66% EER against a semi-informed attacker.

FlipJev: Fast Black-Box Red Teaming Finds Universal Adversarial Suffixes for Typed Decision Models#

Authors: Xiaogeng Liu, Xinyan Wang, Chaowei Xiao Venue: arXiv (2026)

Typed decision models are an emerging class of language models that make decisions instead of generating free-text responses. They answer in a single fast and cheap query and are increasingly used, including in security-sensitive tasks. However, red teaming of these models leaves two gaps: existing adaptive attacks do not use their speed, and few studies have searched for universal adversarial examples. We fill both gaps on Jev, the representative typed decision model, with a quick black-box query-based attack that calls no attacker language model. We conduct extensive experiments on 12 datasets with different data types and application scenarios. The results show that Jev resists common prompt injections: no strategy changes a correct answer in 10,240 paired tests. In contrast, our attack finds short suffixes that flip confident answers within minutes. These suffixes are universal: one suffix per dataset flips 43% of unseen documents. Because typed decision models, unlike classic NLP models, can serve many tasks, we test whether these suffixes transfer across tasks. We find that one suffix generated from one task can flip 90-100% of the samples in other tasks. We therefore believe the evaluation of typed decision models must include strict adaptive attacks to fully reveal their robustness.

Context Compaction for World Models#

Authors: Hayder Tirmazi Venue: arXiv (2026)

World models let observers explore and interact with generated worlds. Recent work shows that when observers revisit a region, current models often fail to regenerate it consistently. We view this as a context retrieval problem. LLM agents manage context retrieval at two layers of their stack. At the model layer, the KV cache design determines how much context fits in memory. At the agent harness layer, harnesses such as Claude Code pick the context sent to the LLM. Prior work on world models has mostly focused only on the model layer. In this paper, we make three contributions. First, we build a benchmark to measure world model context retrieval. It generates new worlds for every episode, preventing a model from recalling rooms using training data. It also separates context retrieval from guesses based on nearby frames. Second, we build an agent harness that performs context compaction for world models, which to our knowledge no prior work has studied. Third, we use our benchmark to study four world model designs. Context compaction lets our world models retrieve rooms up to 4 times beyond their context window without retraining. However, world models with sliding-window attention lose most of their context retrieval when dreaming. Therefore, a dreaming world agent benefits more from extending the reach of its world model using context compaction than switching to sliding window attention at the model layer.

Beyond Aggregate WER: In-domain Adaptation, Learner Speech, Code-Switching, and Speaker Attribution in Online Spanish Learning Classrooms#

Authors: Olga Kellert, Samhitha Harish, Johnatan E Bonilla, Nemika Tyagi, Phanos Kareen, Nelvin Licona-Guevara Venue: arXiv (2026)

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) increasingly supplies transcripts for educational NLP, but aggregate word error rate (WER) can conceal errors concentrated in learner speech, multilingual turns, and speaker attribution. We compare platform-integrated, open-weight, adapted, and commercial ASR configurations on held-out sessions of online L2 Spanish interaction between L1 English learners and their instructors and tutors. Because English is the learner’s shared first language, the interactions contain Spanish-only, English-only, and code-switched turns. Evaluation combines overall WER and character error rate (CER), WER disaggregated by speaker role and language context, and diarization error rate (DER). We also adapt Whisper large-v3 and MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize with LoRA using human-corrected in-domain classroom data. MOSS adaptation produced consistent improvements across speaker roles and language contexts, whereas Whisper showed smaller gains. Domain match proved more important than data volume: Larger out-of-domain bilingual and learner corpora did not outperform the smaller classroom corpus, where learner speech and code-switching are embedded in instructor–learner interaction. The open-weight MOSS adapter was competitive with commercial Muse Voice Transcribe on WER and CER and achieved lower system-provided DER on the recordings eligible for each system. Learner, English-only, and code-switched speech remained more difficult than instructor and Spanish-only speech. The findings demonstrate the value of condition-aware evaluation and interactionally matched data for bilingual educational ASR.

Think Twice: The Metis-1.6 Technical Report#

Authors: Giulianno Vollmer Venue: arXiv (2026)

Metis is a bet that better data, better training methods and new architectures can buy what a bigger parameter count cannot, at a fraction of the cost. Metis-1.6 is its latest step: two language models built from scratch by a single researcher, Praxis (2.94B parameters, 0.46B active per pass) and Logos (10.23B parameters, 1.18B active per pass). Both run a hybrid Mamba-2 and attention mixture-of-experts stack twice for every token, so each token gets a second look. Each was trained on 1.02T tokens in total: 804.76B of pretraining, 18B of context extension to 131,072 tokens, and 200B of supervised fine-tuning. Metis-1.6 was never meant to challenge frontier models, and across 40 post-trained benchmarks it mostly does not: Logos averages 52.93, seventh of 17 models in its comparison set and within a point of Qwen3-4B. But it gets there on roughly a twelfth of the median training data of the comparators that disclose it, and with fewer active parameters per token than any model that beats it, which puts it on the Pareto frontier for both. In mathematics it ranks second of 30 models, with 67.50% on AIME 2025 and 63.64% on HMMT February 2026. It is weak at code, instruction following and long context. The rest of the report is the part I found most valuable: what went wrong. The optimizer I configured never ran, and attention trained with AdamW for all of pretraining. A 1.77B-parameter n-gram memory learned to switch itself off. Five fine-tuning choices cost capability. And a length reward under group-mean normalization punished correct solutions for being long once a group’s pass rate passed 2/3, a sign inversion I prove and fix with a one-line clamp. I close with the 10B-token ablations behind MoRE (Mixture of Recursive Experts), the looped architecture of Metis-1.7.

Classical Verification of Quantum Computation Beyond the Polynomial Hierarchy#

Authors: Atul Mantri Venue: arXiv (2026)

It is not known whether a classical polynomial-time verifier can verify arbitrary polynomial-time quantum computations with an efficient quantum honest prover without computational assumptions. We show that, relative to a classical oracle, such verification is possible for a language beyond the polynomial hierarchy that is decided exactly in quantum polynomial time. The protocol is public-coin, with perfect completeness and soundness error at most 1/4. To our knowledge, no language with such a proof system was previously proved to lie outside AM relative to an oracle. For every unbounded polynomial-time computable function, the oracle can be chosen so that the message count and the oracle query counts of the quantum algorithm, verifier, and honest prover are bounded by this function of the input length. The message count is optimal up to a constant factor, since for a suitable oracle every interactive proof system for the language uses at least a constant fraction of the messages of our protocol on infinitely many lengths, with a constant depending on its verifier. The language is a variant of recursive Fourier sampling in which code tables encode secrets that address data tables. The tables are evaluated recursively, and the oracle stores only the leaves. The quantum algorithm recovers every secret exactly, and the verifier checks the prover’s secrets recursively against random code entries. The recursion restricts the input in a correlated way, as the same secret fixes the code bits and chooses the free coordinate, so Håstad’s switching lemma does not apply. We prove a switching theorem for these restrictions, which may be of independent interest. Designing power electronics converters traditionally requires intensive human expertise across topology sizing, commercial component selection, circuit simulation verification, and schematic drafting. While Large Language Models (LLMs) show promise in electronic design automation (EDA), applying standalone LLMs directly to board-level power electronics schematic synthesis suffers from severe engineering hallucinations, including inaccurate analytical sizing, fabricated or mismatched component part numbers, violated voltage derating margins, and broken topological connectivity. In this paper, we propose a Bounded Dual-Loop Automated Schematic Synthesis Framework that translates natural language specifications into verified, editable KiCad schematics and manufacturing deliverables. Our workflow integrates six stages across three functional columns: (1) requirement parsing and knowledge-driven power stage synthesis covering non-isolated (Buck, Boost) and isolated/resonant (Flyback, DAB, half-bridge LLC) topologies; (2) contract- and evidence-coordinated component selection grounded in distributor APIs and OEM datasheets, coupled with closed-loop ngspice transient verification and stress feedback; and (3) modular control hardware integration and multi-format deliverable generation featuring native KiCad netlist round-trip verification. HyperIntent: Tracking Intent Shifts for Efficient Reasoning on Dynamic Textual Graphs#

Authors: Yilin Yao, Bin Lu, Xinhui Li, Meng Jin, Xinbing Wang Venue: arXiv (2026)

Dynamic web interactions continuously evolve as new edges arrive, offering large language models (LLMs) great potential for reasoning over edge-level temporal semantics. However, continuously arriving edges are often repetitive, obscuring the key structures that truly reflect temporal changes and limiting the efficiency of LLMs. A key insight is that beneath the dense stream of edge arrivals lies a sparse evolution of temporal intent, enabling LLMs to be responsively triggered only when such intent shifts, while otherwise inheriting the previous state. Meanwhile, HyperIntent reduces LLM token consumption by 54.4%-95.9% across six datasets while achieving leading predictive performance. In-depth studies further confirm the necessity of shift triggering for identifying structural and semantic temporal changes. A Lighter QCD-Axion from Small Instantons#

Authors: Sourav Bera, Susobhan Chattopadhyay, Rick S. Gupta Venue: arXiv (2026)

We propose models where the QCD axion mass can be much smaller than the standard expectation. We show that this is possible in models where both QCD and the Standard Model flavor structure get modified in the ultraviolet (UV). In these models, both the axion mass and the up-quark Yukawa can receive sizeable non-perturbative contributions from instantons in the UV. If both these effects are carefully taken into account, the QCD axion is not necessarily heavier than usual expectations and can, in fact, be much lighter. We show that if small instanton contributions account for a large fraction of the up-quark Yukawa, the QCD axion can be lighter than standard expectations whereas it is heavier otherwise. We propose two different models where this condition is realised: (1) one where the QCD gauge group emerges as a subgroup from a product of $SU(3)$ groups in the UV; and (2) a composite Higgs model that also implements partial compositeness. In both these models the QCD axion can lie not only in the region to the right of the QCD axion band but also far to its left. This is especially relevant for a range of proposed haloscope and helioscope experiments that will probe a region mostly to the left of the QCD band.

Improved Gradient Complexity for Quasi-Newton Proximal Extragradient Methods via Certified Curvature Reuse#

Authors: Aryan Mokhtari, Francisco Patitucci Venue: arXiv (2026)

We study how curvature estimated from gradient differences can accelerate proximal-extragradient quasi-Newton methods. Existing global guarantees for QNPE, A-QNPE, and OL-OQN learn Hessian approximations online and incur dimension dependence through regret bounds. We introduce certified curvature reuse: one finite-difference probe per iteration refreshes a group of Hessian columns, while a path-length certificate controls the error of stale columns and determines the step. Each iteration uses at most three gradient queries and one regularized linear solve (or trust-region step), without a gradient line search or an inner oracle-querying loop. In the convex and strongly convex settings, bisection on stored scalar quantities selects a near-maximal certified stepsize; in the nonconvex setting, a uniform certificate gives an explicit learning rate. With other problem parameters fixed, the convex method attains $O(\min{k^{-2},dk^{-3}})$ in a single run, with no logarithmic factor or additive dimension term, matching accelerated gradient descent (AGD) for $k\lesssim d$ and improving on A-QNPE thereafter. In the strongly convex setting with condition number $κ$, a single run matches AGD’s $(1-Θ(κ^{-1/2}))^k$ rate for $k\lesssim d\logκ$ and achieves a superlinear upper envelope, eventually scaling as $(1+k^2/B^2)^{-k}$ with $B=\tilde O(dκ)$, improving on QNPE. In the nonconvex setting, the method finds an $\varepsilon$-stationary point in $O(\min{d^{1/3}\varepsilon^{-3/2},\varepsilon^{-7/4}})$ gradient queries, improving on OL-OQN’s $O(d^{1/4}\varepsilon^{-13/8})$. For sparse Hessians, $d$ is replaced by an effective dimension $χ\le d$. We also compare our bounds with lazy-Hessian, double-loop, and cutting-plane methods.

JT gravity as a conformal scaling limit of random flat combinatorial disks#

Authors: Morris Ang Venue: arXiv (2026)

We prove that flat Jackiw–Teitelboim (JT) gravity arises as a conformal scaling limit of random flat combinatorial disks. We give a clean criterion, formulated in terms of branching explorations, for a discrete model to converge to this conjecturally universal limit. For simplicity, we apply this criterion to a Boltzmann ensemble of disks with rectilinear boundary, defined by summing over combinatorial types and integrating over lengths. As the length of a distinguished side tends to infinity, the rescaled surface converges in law to a continuum random surface prescribed by Ferrari’s path-integral formulation of JT gravity. Convergence is in the topology of smooth convergence of the conformal factors on compact subsets of the interior when uniformizing in a fixed domain; the limiting conformal factor is described by a harmonic Gaussian field closely related to holomorphic multiplicative chaos. The crux is a branching exploration of flat JT gravity whose lengths evolve as conditioned Cauchy processes and whose unexplored components are conditionally independent. We construct this exploration using a conformal welding theorem proved in a companion paper, and show that it is the scaling limit of Budd’s row-by-row exploration of the discrete disks, analogous to the peeling of random planar maps. Under a tightness condition, our criterion upgrades this convergence to a conformal scaling limit for the discrete disks. Other natural combinatorial disk models admit analogous first-row recursions, providing a route to their scaling limits.

Entanglement diagnostics of stochastic-resetting steady states: theory and measurement on superconducting qubits#

Authors: Jakob Murauer, Sabine Tornow, Manas Kulkarni, Eli Barkai, Satya N. Majumdar, Monalisa Singh Roy Venue: arXiv (2026)

Stochastic resetting drives quantum systems into nonequilibrium stationary states unreachable under uninterrupted unitary evolution, and their entanglement depends on whether the reset history is resolved or averaged over. We study this interplay in the transverse-field Ising chain, deriving closed-form results for two qubits and evaluating chains of up to 12 qubits exactly. In the two-qubit system, these closed-form results show that phase matching between energy eigenstates produces resonances, and their visibility in the stationary state is determined by the symmetry of the reset state. Measurements on an IBM superconducting processor show the entropies evaluated before and after the reset average closely following noiseless predictions with no fitted parameter, while concurrence lies below prediction across most of the hardware measurements. The two diagnostics respond differently because they read different components of the same reconstructed state, and a depolarizing model captures the concurrence suppression with one fitted parameter. The difference between the two entropies, i. e., the Jensen gap of entanglement entropy, equals the Holevo information that a subsystem retains about the time since the last reset. With a Bell-state reset, this information grows with chain length across a half-chain cut over the sizes studied, without visible saturation at the smaller reset probabilities. Since reset times are sampled and recorded classically, the trajectory-averaged entropy, and with it the Jensen gap, is obtained without postselection, in contrast to monitored circuits, where trajectory-resolved entanglement requires sampling exponentially rare measurement records.

Left-Handed Transmission Lines for the Dynamical Casimir Effect#

Authors: Valentin E. Salari, Fernando C. Lombardo, Paula I. Villar Venue: arXiv (2026)

Left-handed (LH) superconducting transmission lines, built with series capacitors and shunt inductors instead of the series inductors and shunt capacitors of a conventional right-handed (RH) line, have a spectrum with a nonzero lower band edge and modes that become sparse at high frequencies. We study the dynamical Casimir effect in a LH line terminated by a flux-modulated SQUID and compare it with an RH line built from the same elements and with a long coplanar waveguide terminated by the same SQUID. We derive the quantum Hamiltonian and show that the coupling between two modes factorizes into their amplitudes at the SQUID. Together, the LH spectrum and the mode dependence of this coupling determine which modes are populated and how fast. With equal elements, the LH coupling grows with frequency and exceeds the RH one above a crossover pump frequency set by the cell inductance and capacitance, and only the LH line has resonances above the RH band edge. Pumping two modes at once lets a weakly coupled mode borrow coupling from a strongly coupled one, while crossings of resonance lines populate three or four modes without increasing the growth rate. Compared with the coplanar waveguide, the LH line has a similar coupling but keeps the created particles in the target mode, whereas the nearly equidistant waveguide spreads them over many pairs and needs more pump energy.

Interpreting hidden neurons at the concept level can provide a more meaningful view of what an audio model learns internally. This paper investigates whether hidden neurons in audio classifiers can be associated with human-understandable symbolic acoustic concepts. A model-agnostic neurosymbolic framework is presented for analyzing the final hidden layer of audio classifiers, containing 32 neurons, through symbolic concept induction.

Can an Agent Debug a Web Corpus? Agentic Auditing of Slovak FineWeb2#

Authors: Marek Šuppa, Ivan Vykopal, Jaroslav Kopčan Venue: arXiv (2026)

Multilingual pipelines such as FineWeb2 apply one recipe across a thousand languages, leaving the failure modes of individual languages unanticipated. We use an LLM once, as a corpus debugger rather than a classifier: a tool-using agent explores the corpus through a read-only, budgeted API and compiles each finding into an executable test, which a native speaker then validates. On the 26.5M-document Slovak portion of FineWeb2, three runs costing $2.21-$2.84 each found seven findings over four phenomena. The largest is a machine-generated content farm of 1,408,437 documents (5.32%) that passed the pipeline’s filters, 86.6% of it passing even stricter thresholds. Blinded labels support 6 of the 7 findings (defect rates .53-1.00 against .08 in a random control) and do not support one. The compiled suite scans the full corpus in 0.7 s of wall-clock time without a GPU, against $12k-$90k per LLM classification pass. The best static sampling baseline validates more selectors than any agent run, at under half the cost. The agent contributes exact prevalence, a precision estimate checked against sampled documents, an account of why the pipeline missed each slice, and a reusable test. We release the framework, Slovak test suite, validation data and agent trajectories.

Decentralized Bi-Level Optimization for Cooperative Orbital Transportation of a Rigid Payload#

Authors: Sidhdharth D. Sikka, Revathy Ravuri, Hei Yin Jowett Chan, Tim Echtermeyer, Shaoshuai Mou Venue: arXiv (2026)

The rapid growth of the space economy, alongside increasing orbital congestion and debris, is driving demand for reliable on-orbit transportation of large and non-cooperative objects. Performing such transportation with a swarm of small spacecraft distributes risk and removes the single point of failure inherent to monolithic architectures, but requires solving a non-convex trajectory optimization coupling every agent’s cone-constrained thrust to the payload’s rotational dynamics. Existing approaches span decentralized control laws with asymptotic guarantees but limited constraint enforcement, and centralized and partially centralized planners that reintroduce a single point of failure. In this work, we present a decentralized bi-level framework driven by exact gradients: the problem is split into an inner convex thrust allocation and an outer search over payload torque trajectories. This decomposition yields fuel-efficient solutions quickly, without requiring full NLP convergence, and at short timescales its performance scales with the number of agents. Theoretical convergence guarantees are established, and the method is validated through extensive Monte Carlo simulation, including closed-loop recovery from in-flight faults. These properties make the framework well suited to spacecraft swarm applications.

The Geometry of Metaphor: Probing and Steering Metaphor Representations in Large Language Models#

Authors: Shihui Li, Mette Andersen, Martha Lewis Venue: arXiv (2026)

Metaphor is a crucial aspect of human cognition. As such, we would like to understand how it is represented in large language models (LLMs) and how it might be controlled. We use mechanistic interpretability techniques to identify causal subspaces in a range of LLMs covering different architectures and sizes. We show that we are able to steer output on classification tasks across different parts of speech. We are also able to steer output on generation tasks, indicating that these techniques can be used to control the metaphoricity of LLM output.

Same Entity, Different Script: Diagnosing Cross-Script Entity Matching Failures in Language Models#

Authors: Athira Gopal, Patil Prathamesh Pravin, Ankush Kadu, Arpit Jain, Ashwanth Krishnan Venue: arXiv (2026)

Multilingual language models can give different identity judgments when the same people’s names are written in different scripts. We study this effect using 601 audited identity pairs with attested Devanagari and Latin forms, holding the question and relation fixed. Qwen2.5-7B’s same-person accuracy falls from 35.1% in Latin to 8.5% in Devanagari under Marathi prompts, while different-person accuracy remains above 98%. Evaluation across five models reveals distinct response biases, and reduced tokenization fragmentation does not reliably close the gap. Replicating the design on 374 audited Malayalam pairs built from the same entities reproduces the effect in a second script and language family: same-person accuracy falls to 5.3%, about half the matched Devanagari rate, while the margin still ranks matching above non-matching names at 0.925 AUROC. Latin-trained probes recover label information from Devanagari states, exceeding a string-feature baseline under similarity-matched negatives. Scalar calibration recovers 87% of the raw model-probe balanced-accuracy gap, identifying decision criterion placement as a major contributor. Activation patching localises causal sensitivity to the second entity in Qwen’s middle layers; one shared sensitivity direction transfers about half the full-state margin correction. A fact-independent intervention at this site raises held-out Marathi same-person accuracy from 6.5% to 33.5%, with 98.5% different-person accuracy. Together, these results distinguish script-dependent discrimination from criterion placement and identify a causal site for improving identity judgments.

CrisiLangs: A Multilingual Resource for Crisis Warning Generation#

Authors: Giacomo Gonella, Stefano Menini, Marco Guerini Venue: arXiv (2026)

Communicating with the public during an emergency entails issuing warning messages promptly. In Europe, it often requires producing them in more than one language. Recent work has shown that language models can be trained to generate warning messages as a crisis unfolds, but the available data covers only English. To fill this gap, we present CrisiLangs, a multilingual extension of CrisiText (an English dataset of crisis events paired with warning messages written according to expert communication guidelines) to Italian, Bulgarian, German, and Greek. Expert native speakers translated the warning messages of 1,000 events drawn from 411 scenarios, for a total of 5,000 event-message pairs across five languages. We test the resource to fine-tune a multilingual model on all five languages jointly, with and without the emergency guidelines in the prompt, and compare it with a zero-shot baseline. The fine-tuned models outperform the baseline on ROUGE-L, BLEU and BERTScore in every language and configuration. In a human preference study, low decoding temperatures receive the most preferences in all five languages and in both configurations, while the effect of the guidelines varies by language.

Spark-Audio 1.0 Technical Report#

Authors: Chenyue Zhang, Cong Liu, Dingshu Tian, Dong Sun, Genshun Wan, Guanming Meng, Guolong Zhong, Hang Chen, Hankun Yang, Haoming Wang, Haoyang Wang, Hengquan Mei, Huaxin Wu, Jiahao Li, Jiahuan Fan, Jiamin Ma, Jianqing Gao, Jigang Tang, Jin Liu, Jingchen Li, Jinjie Fu, Kaixuan Guo, Kun Song, Li Xu, Li Zhang, Lijuan Liu, Liyan Luo, Ming Sheng, Mingen Zhao, Mingting Hu, Ningqian Wu, Pan Xu, Pei Xu, Qiangyu Tan, Rui Wang, Ruzi Li, Saier Jin, Shibo Xu, Shifu Xiong, Shihao Jiang, Tan Liu, Wei Wu, Wei Xu, Wenyu Wang, Xiangyu Jia, Xiao Li, Xiaowen Duan, Xiaoxue Guo, Xin Chen, Xinyi Liang, Xu Chen, Yanan Song, Yang Xu, Yexia Huang, Yibo Li, Yifan Xu, Yikang Liu, Yikun Li, Yiting Lin, Yi Fang, Yuxin Wang, Zan Feng, Zhenchuan Li, Zhuo Chen, Zichao Zhang, Ziwen Wang Venue: arXiv (2026)

This paper introduces Spark-Audio 1.0, a speech foundation model trained on domestically produced computing infrastructure. The model accepts text and speech inputs and generates text outputs. It supports multilingual (99 languages) and multi-dialect (202 dialects) speech recognition, speech translation, audio perception and understanding, and both text- and speech-based dialogue, marking a shift from merely hearing clearly'' to genuinely understanding’’ speech. Architecturally, Spark-Audio 1.0 couples a 0.65B-parameter dense audio encoder with a 30B-A3B Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) large language model (LLM). The audio encoder is pretrained with representation-enhanced BEST-RQ and then refined through multi-task supervised training. The audio–LLM is trained through staged pretraining, followed by post-training that combines supervised fine-tuning with on-policy distillation. In total, the model is trained on 13 million hours of audio data together with large-scale text data. We evaluate Spark-Audio 1.0 on mainstream audio and text benchmarks, as well as on in-house test sets drawn from real-world application scenarios. Despite using substantially less audio training data than Qwen3.5-Omni-Flash, Spark-Audio 1.0 achieves comparable performance on most audio and text tasks, outperforms it in multilingual speech recognition, spoken dialogue, and real-world application scenarios, and approaches the performance of larger closed-source models. Nevertheless, there remains room for improvement in instruction following and complex audio reasoning. Finally, the successful training of Spark-Audio 1.0 on domestic hardware, including Huawei Ascend 910B and Hygon BW1000 chips, demonstrates the feasibility of a domestic technical pathway for building speech foundation models.

Bilingual Language Models Capture Cross-Linguistic Influence in Second-Language Reading#

Authors: Abderrahmane Issam, Irene E. Winther, Stefan L. Frank Venue: arXiv (2026)

While monolingual language model (LM) surprisal is widely established as a predictor of reading times, it cannot capture the non-native (L2) reading dynamics that are shaped by the reader’s first language (L1). In this work, we investigate whether the cross-lingual transfer within bilingual models aligns with human L1-driven biases during L2 reading. We evaluate bilingual models on eye-tracking data of readers from 12 diverse L1 backgrounds reading English texts. To isolate L1-specific transfer, we compare bilingual models trained on an L1 that matches the readers’ L1 background to both a monolingual English baseline and bilingual models trained on other L1s. We demonstrate that matching the readers’ L1 improves the prediction of L2 reading times. This predictive advantage is concentrated in late-stage eye-tracking measures, modulated by L1-L2 script overlap, and peaks among intermediate-proficiency readers. These findings provide evidence that bilingual LMs capture aspects of cross-linguistic influence, and that such influence is present in L2 reading behavior.

Once a Tree, Always a Tree? Cross-lingual Transfer of Hierarchical Generalization in Language Models#

Authors: Shin Yamaguchi, Masaya Taniguchi, Tatsuki Kuribayashi, Taro Watanabe, Yoshinobu Kano Venue: arXiv (2026)

Language models (LMs) often exhibit successful cross-lingual transfer through continued (pre)training, still their underlying process remains unclear. One possibility is that LMs can leverage the evidence toward language-universal principles, namely, hierarchical structure, from a source language (L1) to a new language (L2), while many other non-linguistic explanations, e.g., improved initialization of position embeddings, can be potentially possible. In this study, to quantify the cross-linguistic transferability of linguistic evidence in cross-lingual LM training, we introduce a new experimental design, namely, a cross-lingual poverty-of-stimulus (CPOS) setting that ablates how LMs’ hierarchical generalization in L2 can be led by structural evidence provided in L1. We contrast counterfactual L1 and L2 learning scenarios where the L2 corpus is ambiguous between linear and hierarchical grammar rules, while the amount of positive evidence for the hierarchical rule is controlled on the L1 side. Our experimental results show that LMs can partially, yet not perfectly, transfer L1 structural evidence to L2, yielding only a small but detectable hierarchical bias, while the strength of transfer depends on language pairs. We further ablate the conditions for successful transfer, motivated by recent reports on the substantial impact of vocabulary overlap. We indeed find that vocabulary overlap is crucial even for the cross-lingual transfer of hierarchical syntactic generalization. This suggests that this also explains the language-pair-dependent tendencies in the main experiments and suggests that the transfer of linguistic evidence in continued new-language learning is only conditionally achievable.

Meaning Contracts: Local Semantic Geometry in Large Language Models#

Authors: Andrés Daniel Godoy Ortiz Venue: arXiv (2026)

This paper studies the local geometry of semantic meaning in large language models. Current representation analyses ask whether meanings are close in embedding space, or whether linear probes separate semantic classes, but they do not characterize the within-concept local geometry: what is invariant across realizations of a single semantic factor, what varies, and what changes when meaning is refined. This paper characterizes that geometry through two controlled probes evaluated on four architecturally diverse decoder-only LLMs (Gemma 4, Llama 3.2, Qwen 2.5, Pythia). A cross-lingual probe uses language as a perturbation to separate exact meaning matches, same-factor realizations, nearby concepts, and out-of-domain contrasts. A refinement probe separates specificity-bearing semantic narrowing from high-probability attributive elaboration and adversarial controls; both probes are validated by blind human judgments. Four findings recur across all models: under mean pooling, once dominant variance directions are removed, sentence pairs order by semantic relatedness (same factor, then same domain, then different domain) without overlap over a band of layers; semantic factors organize around an invariant core surrounded by structured realization-specific variation; refinement produces directed displacement into tighter neighborhoods, including against controls built on the same base noun; and sentence-level and token-local readouts expose complementary geometry (volume contraction at the sentence level, sharper anisotropic endpoints at token-local readout). We interpret these results as evidence that LLM semantics are organized as local anisotropic neighborhoods in which meaning contracts under refinement.

Task-Preserving Multilingual Instruction Tuning for Open-Ended QA in WMT 2026 MIST#

Authors: Aleix Sant, Jordi Luque, Carlos Escolano Venue: arXiv (2026)

This paper describes our submission to the WMT 2026 Multilingual Instruction Shared Task. We adapt Qwen3-8B with LoRA using organiser-provided data, EuroAlpaca, a multilingual instruction dataset created through task-preserving localisation, and CrossEuroAlpaca, a cross-lingual augmentation that assigns different source and target languages to instructions, contexts and responses. Our system targets open-ended question answering (QA-OEG) in ten shared-task languages represented in the auxiliary data. Under matched decoding, adaptation improves the mean Gemma-4 judge score on target-language QA-OEG by 7.8% relative to the base model. On an additional evaluation using the Aya Evaluation Suite across all shared-task languages, it yields relative gains of 41.5% in macro-averaged ROUGE-L and 2.9% in BERTScore-F1, with larger improvements in target languages. However, these gains are task- and language-specific. Gemma-4 scores improve for QA-OEG and summarisation in the target languages but decline for context-grounded QA there and across all three tasks in the non-target languages.

Scaling Multilingual Pretraining of Pixel Language Models#

Authors: Ilker Kesen, Jenny Kunz, Kasra Malihi, Desmond Elliott Venue: arXiv (2026)

Pixel language models process text as rendered images instead of a finite vocabulary, which gives them strong cross-lingual transfer abilities. However, not much is known about how these abilities are influenced by larger-scale pretraining. In this paper, we train a family of MPIXEL models along three axes of variation: more languages, more scripts, and larger model capacity under fixed per-language compute. In evaluations on five tasks covering 83 languages, we find that scaling acts locally: each axis improves the added languages and scripts, while increased capacity mitigates the curse of multilinguality from pretraining on more languages. Transfer to unseen scripts improves with multilingual pretraining, but does not further improve with scaling. On typologically distant low-resource languages, our models outperform subword and character-level baselines across many writing systems. We also show that larger pixel models reserve more layers for visual processing, and that cross-lingual alignment weakens as more languages are added. Overall, our findings characterize how multilingual scaling improves pixel language models.

Closed-Loop Dual-Reference Guidance for Accent Control in Cross-Lingual Voice Cloning#

Authors: Yuanzhong Zheng, Wei Huang, Ming Ma, Kailun Jin, Youhai Jiang Venue: arXiv (2026)

Zero-shot cross-lingual voice cloning often carries the reference speaker’s accent into the target language. We propose closed-loop dual-reference guidance, which pairs the original reference with a reusable native anchor constructed by voice conversion (VC). The references initialize separate language-model (LM) states that guide a shared acoustic trajectory; each completed patch then updates both states, keeping their generated histories aligned with the emitted speech. Anchor construction runs once per speaker, and online synthesis uses a frozen TTS model without per-utterance VC. On 20 held-out French speakers, the fixed setting $λ^{!*}=0.33$ reduces residual French LID mass by 73% versus direct cloning, at an ECAPA similarity cost of only $0.042$; relative to anchor-only cloning, it recovers $0.155$. The method achieves the lowest mean WER ($0.009$) and highest mean GOP ($4.750$) among the held-out test arms. In blinded preference tests, native-English listeners favor it over direct cloning on pronunciation naturalness, and over both anchor-only cloning and a TTS$\to$VC cascade on speaker identity. Online RTF is approximately 26% lower than the cascade’s ($0.59$ vs. $0.80$), and the same setting reduces Spanish LID mass by 56% without re-tuning.

How Well Do Decision Models Transfer to Vietnamese? An Empirical Study of Text Classification#

Authors: Phat Huu Ha, Nguyen Xuan Phuc, Nong Nhut Quy, Trinh Tran Tran, Nguyen Minh Triet, Dang Van Thin Venue: arXiv (2026)

Recent advances in specialized decision models have expanded the possibilities for zero-shot classification, yet their effectiveness on Vietnamese remains underexplored. This study evaluates four specialized decision models across 13 classification settings spanning six task groups, examining zero-shot performance, sensitivity to label-description language, and robustness to Vietnamese linguistic variations. The evaluation considers English, Vietnamese, and bilingual label descriptions, alongside common linguistic perturbations involving teencode, typographical errors, and diacritic removal. Under English label descriptions, JEV achieves the strongest overall performance, ranking first in 11 classification settings. The effects of label-description language vary across models and datasets, with bilingual descriptions providing no consistent improvement. Robustness experiments reveal that diacritic removal causes the most substantial performance degradation, reducing mean macro-$F_1$ by 9.51–15.47 points across model configurations, considerably more than teencode or typographical errors. These findings highlight the importance of label-description choices and Vietnamese orthographic variation when evaluating specialized decision models for zero-shot text classification.

IndoNutriQA: An Indonesian Clinical Nutrition Question Answering Benchmark#

Authors: Tirana Noor Fatyanosa, Ummu Ditya Erliana, Mahendra Data, Muhammad Nurwegiono, Eleazar Tadeo Eman, Alham Fikri Aji Venue: arXiv (2026)

Clinical Question Answering (QA) underpins Clinical Intelligent Decision Support Systems, but progress is severely limited for low-resource languages such as Indonesian by the absence of public domain-specific data. We introduce IndoNutriQA, an expert-curated Indonesian clinical QA benchmark of 911 question-answer pairs built from real clinical-nutrition reports. Rather than a large-scale training corpus, it is positioned as a high-quality evaluation resource: every pair is grounded in a clinically relevant context, enriched with structured metadata (anthropometric assessment, medical diagnosis, document provenance, chunk segmentation). Eight standardised question types following the Nutrition Care Process (NCP), including the Problem, Etiology, Sign and Symptom (PES) diagnosis statement, are answered for every report, which was written by a graduate-level dietetic intern, by one of three nutritionists. Answers are free-form clinical prose rather than extractive spans, and an expert validation study finds 6-10% of reference answers contestable. The dataset supports generative, extractive and retrieval-augmented QA as well as zero- and few-shot LLM evaluation. We report fine-tuned baselines (IndoBERT variants, mT5, RAG) with 5-fold cross-validation, learning curves and bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, and benchmark eight contemporary LLMs zero- and five-shot. We further calibrate our LLM-as-judge rubric against three expert raters (one RD, two nutritionists), finding it human-comparable on factuality and completeness but unreliable on clinical safety, and a human error analysis shows it misses unsupported content that the raters identify. IndoNutriQA is released to foster research in Indonesian clinical NLP and nutritional decision support.

Climbing the Ladder: Automating Data Pipelines and Reproducing Baselines in Low-Resource Settings#

Authors: Donato Meoli, Eryk Wdowiak Venue: arXiv (2026)

The massively multilingual translation systems that now include Sicilian learned it from noisy, non-standardised web text. To obtain better translation quality, we redevelop the small neural machine translation (NMT) system that Wdowiak customized for Sicilian. We rebuild on maintained software the extraction of the parallel text from the PDFs of the bilingual journal Arba Sicula, whose sentences are aligned by a language-agnostic sentence encoder with thresholds tuned against an existing hand alignment and then filtered for the typical errors of automatic alignment. Furthermore, the tokenization and the desinence-biased subword segmentation of the original system are ported to a Perl-free implementation, and all the results are measured on a frozen test set drawn from the journal. We compare small Transformers trained from scratch with the pretrained NLLB model adapted by low-rank fine-tuning, in both directions and with Italian as a closely related bridge language, and we adapt to the pretrained model the reverse-training recipe of the original system, i.e., a stage on forward-scored back-translations of the web Sicilian followed by a stage on the curated literary text. The pretrained model is by far the better of the two, and the staged training improves the translation into Sicilian more than the translation from it. On the test set of the original system, and in the tokenized space in which it was scored, the staged model with a larger adapter and more back-translations is above the reverse-training recipe in the directions that involve English and below it between Sicilian and Italian. Yet, a multilingual adapter degrades the directions on which it was not trained, which limits its use as a general multilingual model.

SyRA-CS: Synthetic-to-Real Adaptation for Code-Switched ASR with Meta-Learning#

Authors: Fadel Thior, Ahmer Tabassum, Haania Siddiqui, Hanan Aldarmaki, Thamar Solorio Venue: arXiv (2026)

Code-switched (CS) automatic speech recognition (ASR) remains difficult because real CS speech is scarce and synthetic speech does not fully match it. We investigate whether synthetic CS speech is more useful for learning an adaptation-friendly initialization than for ordinary continual fine-tuning. We propose SyRA-CS, a pair-specific synthetic-to-real adaptation framework based on Reptile meta-learning. For each language pair and pretrained model, we meta-train on the corresponding synthetic CS-FLEURS subset and then adapt using one hour of labeled real target speech. We evaluate four architectural settings: Encoder-CTC, FastConformer-RNN-T, Attention Encoder-Decoder (AED) and Speech-LLM. Experiments cover on Arabic-, French-, German-, and Hindi-English, against synthetic-only transfer, real-only fine-tuning, and continual fine-tuning. SyRA-CS improves over continual fine-tuning on WER in all 16 model-pair comparisons and on CER in 15 of 16, with relative macro WER reductions of 17.7% for XLS-R and 5.3-5.8% for the other three models. The gains hold across the tested models but vary in size across architectures, language pairs and metrics.

Automating Feature Flag Cleanup at Scale with Multi-Agent LLM Systems: An Industrial Experience at DoorDash#

Authors: Atharv Chandratre, Jai Datar Venue: arXiv (2026)

Feature flags are runtime configuration mechanisms used for gradual feature rollouts, experimentation, and kill switches. Once a feature is fully rolled out, the flag and its gated code become technical debt and can introduce operational risk through inadvertently enabling dormant code paths. At DoorDash, over 56,000 feature flags span 623 repositories, with 1,659 currently stale as of May 2026, with internal estimates of one to two hours per manual cleanup. Existing rule-based cleanup tools proved insufficient in DoorDash’s codebases.
